Overview

In the last few labs, we compared groups. A correlation asks a different question: do two variables go together? Pearson's correlation (r) tells us the strength and direction of the relationship between two variables, and whether that relationship is statistically significant.

In the class survey, you completed several different measures. We'll examine four of them: stress, procrastination, self-esteem, and age. Past research gives us some reasons to expect these variables to be related:

  • Stress may encourage procrastination, and also be a result of it (Sirois, 2023).
  • Stress is associated with self-esteem in several ways (Galanakis et al., 2016).
  • Lower self-esteem is associated with increased procrastination (Hajloo, 2014; Yang et al., 2023).
  • Stress levels are high between ages 20 and 50, but begin to decline around age 70 (Stone et al., 2017).
  • People tend to procrastinate less as they move through adulthood (Baulke et al., 2026).
  • Self-esteem increases throughout adulthood, but then starts to decline (Orth et al., 2010).

Learning objectives — by the end of this lab you should be able to:

Part 3

Understanding the output

The correlation matrix

Each pair of variables meets in a cell of the matrix. For each correlation, three values are reported:

  • Pearson's r — the correlation coefficient.
  • Degrees of freedom — equal to the number of participants minus 2 (n − 2).
  • p value — the correlation is significant if p < .05. If p > .05, we fail to reject the null hypothesis that there is no relationship between the variables, so the correlation is non-significant. Because we checked “Flag significant correlations,” jamovi marks the significant ones for you.

The asterisks come from the “Flag significant correlations” option: * means p < .05, ** means p < .01, and *** means p < .001. Also notice that the degrees of freedom aren't identical for every pair. That's because a few participants are missing a value on some variables, so jamovi uses all the participants it has for each pair.

The scatterplots

Each scatterplot shows every participant as a dot, positioned by their scores on the two variables, with a line of best fit running through the cloud. When you look at one, ask two questions:

  • Form — do the dots follow a roughly straight line, or something else, like a curve?
  • Direction — as one variable goes up, does the other tend to go up (a positive relationship) or down (a negative relationship)?

Strength and direction

The sign of r tells you the direction. The size of r (how far it is from 0) tells you the strength: the closer to 0, the weaker the relationship, and the closer to ±1, the stronger.

In this class, we use these cutoffs to describe the strength of a correlation:

Size of rStrength
Greater than 0, up to .3Weak
.3 up to .5Moderate
.5 or higherStrong

Use the size of r without its sign when you judge strength: r = −.40 is just as strong as r = .40. The sign only tells you the direction.

The coefficient of determination (R2)

Square the correlation coefficient to get R2. It tells you the proportion of variance the two variables share. Multiply by 100 to get a percentage. For example, R2 = .05 means that 5% of the variance in one variable is shared with the other.

Part 4

Reporting a correlation

For each pair of variables, you need to report:

  1. The correlation coefficient (r)
  2. The strength and direction
  3. Whether the correlation is statistically significant
  4. The coefficient of determination (R2)
  5. An APA-formatted sentence reporting the results

Writing the APA-formatted sentence

To write the sentence, you need the strength, direction, and significance; Pearson's r; the degrees of freedom; and the p value. The sentence has two forms.

If the correlation is significant (p < .05):

"A significant, [strength], [direction] Pearson's correlation indicated that as [variable 1] increased, [variable 2] [increased / decreased], r(df) = ___, p = ___."

If the correlation is non-significant (p > .05):

"A non-significant Pearson's correlation indicated no association between [variable 1] and [variable 2], r(df) = ___, p = ___."

Remember: statistical symbols (r, p) are always italicized. If jamovi reports p < .001, write p < .001.

A worked example: stress and procrastination

  1. r = 0.240
  2. Strength and direction: weak and positive — as stress increases, so does procrastination.
  3. Significant? Yes. p < .001, which is less than .05 (jamovi flags it with ***).
  4. R2 = (0.240)2 = 0.058, so about 6% of the variance in stress is shared with procrastination.
  5. APA sentence: "A significant, weak, positive Pearson's correlation indicated that as stress increased, procrastination increased, r(206) = 0.24, p < .001."
