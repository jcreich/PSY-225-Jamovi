Overview

So far, every analysis we've run has described our sample on its own. A one-sample t-test is the first test where we compare our sample against something outside it — a known or expected value from the population — to ask whether our sample's average is genuinely different, or just different by chance.

We'll test whether psychology students (our sample) differ from the general population on three personality traits from the Dark Triad — a set of three related but distinct personality traits, each associated with a tendency toward self-serving and interpersonally harmful behavior. Each trait has a known average in the general population, shown below.

Part 3

Understanding the output

Let's talk through the output you generated in Part 2, starting from the bottom.

Descriptives

This tells you about your sample: its size (n), mean, standard deviation, and standard error — which is just the sample SD divided by the square root of n.

Normality test (Shapiro-Wilk)

This tests the assumption that your data are normally distributed. The test statistic is W, and what matters for you is the p-value: if p < .05, you violate the assumption of normality. If p > .05, you meet it.

One-Sample T-Test

  • Statistic — your observed t value. This is what you'd get by hand: (sample mean − population mean) ÷ standard error.
  • df — degrees of freedom, equal to n − 1.
  • p — the probability of your results if the null hypothesis were true. If p < .05 (or whatever alpha you're using), you reject the null — your sample is significantly different from the population.
  • Mean difference — sample mean minus population mean.
  • Effect size (Cohen's d) — how big the difference is, in standard deviation units. The sign tells you the direction (negative = sample below population); the size (reported as an absolute value) tells you the magnitude — conventionally, around 0.2 is small, 0.5 is medium, and 0.8 is large.

Part 4

Writing an APA-formatted sentence

A complete APA-style sentence for a one-sample t-test needs:

  • The mean and SD of the sample
  • The mean of the population
  • The observed t value
  • Degrees of freedom
  • The p-value
  • Cohen's d (always reported as an absolute value)

Remember: statistical symbols (M, SD, t, p, d) are always italicized.

It follows this template:

"[Sample]'s average [variable] score (M = ___, SD = ___) significantly differs from the average in the general population (μ = ___), t(___) = ___, p = ___, d = ___."
