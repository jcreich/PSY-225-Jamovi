Overview

An independent-samples t-test answers a simple question: do two groups differ in their average on a single variable? Unlike the related-samples test from the last lab, the two sets of scores come from different people — each participant belongs to one group or the other, never both.

Our example asks: does having siblings affect your personality? “Only child syndrome” (a theory associated with G. Stanley Hall) suggests that only children are prone to certain characteristics, such as being spoiled, selfish, maladjusted, bossy, antisocial, and lonely. The idea has made its way into popular culture, but it lacks clear empirical support. So let's test it: does being an only child affect personality? We'll start with extraversion and then try agreeableness and neuroticism on your own.

Part 2

Running an independent-samples t-test

Let's walk through extraversion together. The sibling variable is coded 1 = YES (has siblings) and 2 = NO (does not have siblings), and jamovi will show those labels in your output.

  1. Make sure you're in the Analyses tab.
  2. In the ribbon, click T-Tests → Independent Samples T-Test. The Analysis and Output windows should open.
  3. In the Analysis window, move the dependent variable (EXTRAVERSION_AVG) to the Dependent Variables box, and the sibling variable (SIBLING) to the Grouping Variable box.
  4. Under Hypothesis, keep the default directionality (Group 1 ≠ Group 2) — we're running a two-tailed test.
  5. Under Additional Statistics, check Mean difference, Effect size, and Descriptives.
  6. Under Assumption Checks, check Homogeneity test and Normality test.
  7. Your output is ready!

Reminder: you don't need to capture a screenshot for this extraversion example. For the other traits in Parts 5 and 6 (and the extra credit), you will — so once your output is ready, copy or screenshot the entire output before moving on.

Which group is Group 1? jamovi puts the group with the lower code first. Here, that's YES (the group coded 1: students with siblings), so the mean difference is YES − NO (with siblings − without siblings). A positive mean difference (and a positive d) means students with siblings scored higher; a negative one means students without siblings scored higher.

Here's what you should get:

Both assumption tests are significant here. Shapiro-Wilk p < .001 means we violate the assumption of normality, and Levene's p = .038 (less than .05) means we violate the assumption of equal variances — jamovi even adds a footnote (the small “a” next to the t statistic) to warn you about that. Remember: in this class, we interpret our results the same way regardless.

Now the t-test itself. The t-test p = .934, which is much greater than .05, so we fail to reject the null hypothesis: students with and without siblings do not differ significantly in extraversion. Cohen's d is 0.02, which is essentially no difference at all — students with siblings scored just 0.02 standard deviations higher.

Model APA sentence: "Students with a sibling (M = 3.07, SD = 1.02) did not report significantly different levels of extraversion compared to students without a sibling (M = 3.05, SD = 0.77), t(206) = 0.08, p = .93, d = 0.02."

Part 3

Understanding the output

Let's talk through the output you generated in Part 2, starting from the bottom.

Group descriptives

This tells you about your sample. Because there are two groups, you get two sets of descriptives — one for students with a sibling and one for students without: each group's sample size (N), mean, and standard deviation. (You also get a standard error for each group. It isn't the same standard error we calculate in class, so don't worry about it!)

Assumption checks

There are two assumptions to check, and for both of them the rule is the same: look at the p-value.

  • Normality test (Shapiro-Wilk) — the statistic is W. If p < .05, you violate the assumption of normality. If p > .05, you meet it.
  • Homogeneity of variances test (Levene's) — the statistic is F. If p < .05, you violate the assumption that the two groups have equal variances. If p > .05, you meet it.

If Levene's test is significant, the usual fix is to apply a correction (the Welch correction). In this class, regardless of whether it's significant or not, we'll interpret our results the same way. And if we were running this test for anything other than practice, we'd want to do something about a violated normality assumption, too.

Independent Samples T-Test

  • Statistic — your observed t value: the difference between the two group means divided by the standard error of the difference.
  • df — degrees of freedom, equal to the total sample size minus 2 (n1 + n2 − 2).
  • p — the probability of results like yours if the null hypothesis (no difference between the groups) were true. jamovi gives you the exact probability rather than asking you to compare tobs to tcrit. If p < .05 (or whatever alpha you're using), you reject the null — the group means are significantly different. If not, you fail to reject the null.
  • Mean difference — the difference between the two group means (Group 1 − Group 2). Check its sign against the group coding!
  • SE difference — the standard error of the distribution of differences.
  • Effect size (Cohen's d) — how big the difference is, in standard deviation units. A positive value means Group 1 scored higher; the size tells you the magnitude — conventionally, around 0.2 is small, 0.5 is medium, and 0.8 is large.

Three different p-values. Your output has one in the Shapiro-Wilk table, one in Levene's table, and one in the t-test table. The first two check assumptions. Only the t-test p answers your research question — it's the one you use to decide whether to reject the null.

Part 4

Writing an APA-formatted sentence

A complete APA-style sentence for an independent-samples t-test needs:

  • The mean and SD of each group
  • The observed t value
  • Degrees of freedom (the total)
  • The p-value
  • Cohen's d (always reported as an absolute value)

Remember: statistical symbols (M, SD, t, p, d) are always italicized.

It follows this template:

"Students with a sibling (M = ___, SD = ___) [did / did not] report significantly different levels of [trait] compared to students without a sibling (M = ___, SD = ___), t(___) = ___, p = ___, d = ___."

If your result is significant, you can say which group was higher, for example: "Students with a sibling reported significantly higher levels of …"
