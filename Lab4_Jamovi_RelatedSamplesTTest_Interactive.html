Overview

In the last lab, we compared one sample to a known population value. Here, we compare two sets of scores that come from the same people. This is a related-samples (also called paired-samples or dependent-samples) t-test, and it's the right tool whenever you want to know:

  • Have scores from the same person changed?
  • Are scores from the same person different across two conditions?
  • Are scores from matched pairs different?

Our example asks: does facial hair influence our perception of a person's qualities? Specifically, did participants rate Paul Rudd differently when he had a full beard compared to when he was beardless? Every participant rated both versions, on three qualities: sociable, intelligent, and kind.

Because each person gives us a pair of scores, the test works on the difference between the two scores within each person, and asks whether those differences are, on average, different from zero.

  • Run a related-samples (paired-samples) t-test in jamovi
  • Check and interpret the assumption of normality (Shapiro-Wilk test)
  • Interpret every piece of the output, including how the order you enter the variables determines the direction of the difference
  • Write a complete, correctly formatted APA-style results sentence

Part 2

Running a related-samples t-test

Let's walk through the sociable ratings together.

  1. Make sure you're in the Analyses tab.
  2. In the ribbon, click T-Tests → Paired Samples T-Test. The Analysis and Output windows should open.
  3. In the Analysis window, move both sociable variables (SOCIAL_NOBEARD and SOCIAL_BEARD) to the Paired Variables box, beardless first.
  4. Under Hypothesis, keep the default directionality (Measure 1 ≠ Measure 2) — we're running a two-tailed test.
  5. Under Additional Statistics, check Mean difference, Effect size, and Descriptives.
  6. Under Assumption Checks, check Normality test.
  7. Your output is ready! Copy and paste (or screenshot) the entire output before proceeding.

The order matters. The order you enter the two variables determines how the difference scores are created. Entering SOCIAL_NOBEARD first means jamovi computes Beardless − Beard for every participant, so a positive mean difference means beardless Paul Rudd was rated higher, and a negative one means bearded Paul Rudd was rated higher.

Here's what you should get:

The Shapiro-Wilk p is < .001 (less than .05), so the difference scores violate the assumption of normality. If we were running this test for anything other than practice, we'd want to do something about that.

The t-test p is also < .001, so we reject the null hypothesis: sociable ratings were significantly different for beardless and bearded Paul Rudd. (Two different tables, two different questions: the first p checks an assumption, the second answers the research question.)

Model APA sentence: "Participants rated Paul Rudd as significantly more sociable when he was beardless (M = 3.79, SD = 0.86) compared to when he had a beard (M = 3.60, SD = 0.86), t(207) = 3.50, p < .001, d = 0.24."

Part 3

Understanding the output

Let's talk through the output you generated in Part 2, starting from the bottom.

Descriptives

This tells you about your sample. Because there are two sets of ratings, you get two sets of descriptives — one for Paul Rudd without a beard and one for Paul Rudd with a beard: the sample size (n, the number of pairs), mean, and standard deviation for each.

Normality test (Shapiro-Wilk)

This tests the assumption of normality. For a related-samples t-test, the assumption is about the difference scores, so jamovi runs the test on the differences between the two measures (you'll see both variable names in the row). The test statistic is W, and what matters for you is the p-value: if p < .05, you violate the assumption of normality. If p > .05, you meet it.

Paired Samples T-Test

  • Statistic — your observed t value: the mean difference divided by the standard error of the difference.
  • df — degrees of freedom, equal to the number of pairs minus 1 (n − 1).
  • p — the probability of results like yours if the null hypothesis (no difference) were true. If p < .05 (or whatever alpha you're using), you reject the null — the two sets of ratings are significantly different.
  • Mean difference — the average of the difference scores, which is also the difference between the two means (Beardless − Beard). Check its sign against the order you entered the variables!
  • SE difference — the standard error of the distribution of differences.
  • Effect size (Cohen's d) — how big the difference is, in standard deviation units. The sign tells you the direction (a positive value means the first variable you entered was rated higher); the size tells you the magnitude — conventionally, around 0.2 is small, 0.5 is medium, and 0.8 is large.

Two different p-values. Your output has one in the Shapiro-Wilk table and one in the t-test table. The Shapiro-Wilk p checks an assumption. The t-test p answers your research question — it's the one you use to decide whether to reject the null.

For our sociable example, a plain-language interpretation of d sounds like this: "Beardless Paul Rudd was rated 0.24 standard deviations higher on sociability than bearded Paul Rudd" — a small effect.

Part 4

Writing an APA-formatted sentence

A complete APA-style sentence for a related-samples t-test needs:

  • The mean and SD of the sample for each condition
  • The observed t value
  • Degrees of freedom
  • The p-value
  • Cohen's d (always reported as an absolute value)

Remember: statistical symbols (M, SD, t, p, d) are always italicized.

It follows this template:

"Participants rated Paul Rudd as significantly [more / less] [quality] when he was [condition] (M = ___, SD = ___) compared to when he was [other condition] (M = ___, SD = ___), t(___) = ___, p = ___, d = ___."

If your result is not significant, say so: the ratings "did not significantly differ" between conditions, and report the same statistics.
